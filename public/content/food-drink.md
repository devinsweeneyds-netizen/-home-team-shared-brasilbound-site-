# Food & Drink: A Culinary Tour of Brazil

Brazilian cuisine is a vibrant fusion of Indigenous, African, and European (mostly Portuguese) influences. It is hearty, diverse, and generally very high quality. Here’s what every American traveler should know about eating and drinking in the country.

### Must-Try Dishes
- **Feijoada**: The national dish. A rich black bean stew with various cuts of pork (ribs, sausage, jerky). It is traditionally served on **Saturdays** with white rice, collard greens (*couve*), farofa (toasted manioc flour), and orange slices.
- **Churrasco**: Brazilian BBQ. For the ultimate experience, go to a **Rodízio** (all-you-can-eat steakhouse) where waiters bring skewers of meat to your table. Try the **Picanha** (prime sirloin cap)—it is the prized cut of Brazil.
- **Coxinha**: The ultimate street food. A teardrop-shaped fried dough filled with shredded seasoned chicken (and often cream cheese, or *catupiry*).
- **Pão de Queijo**: Cheese bread made with tapioca flour. It’s naturally gluten-free, chewy, and addictive. Best enjoyed hot from a "Casa do Pão de Queijo."
- **Açaí**: In the US, it’s a health food. In Brazil, it’s a delicious, frozen treat often served in a bowl with granola and bananas. Note that in the North (Amazon), it’s often eaten savory with fried fish.

### Exotic Fruits & Juices
One of the highlights of Brazil is the **Sucos** (juice) bars found on almost every corner.
- Try fruits you’ve never heard of: **Cupuaçu**, **Caju** (cashew fruit), **Graviola**, and **Acerola**.
- **Guaraná Antarctica**: The national soda. It tastes like a mix of apple and berry and contains natural caffeine from the Guaraná berry.

### The Drinks
- **Caipirinha**: The national cocktail. Made with **Cachaça** (sugarcane spirit), lime, and sugar. Be careful: they are delicious but very strong. Variations with strawberry (*caipimorangos*) or passion fruit (*caipifrutas*) are also popular.
- **Coffee**: Brazil is the world’s largest coffee producer. Most places serve a "cafezinho"—a small, intense, and usually very sweet black coffee. If you want a US-style latte, ask for a "café com leite."

### Dietary Restrictions
- **Gluten-Free**: Brazil is surprisingly friendly for Celiacs. Many staples like **Farofa**, **Pão de Queijo**, and **Tapioca** (a crêpe made from manioc starch) are naturally gluten-free.
- **Vegetarian/Vegan**: In big cities, this is easy. In smaller towns, "vegetarian" might be interpreted as "only chicken." Look for "Self-Service" (kilo) restaurants; they have massive salad bars and many hot vegetable options.

### Safety & Health
- **Water**: As mentioned, stick to bottled water. 
- **Street Food**: Generally safe if you see a high turnover of customers. The *Acarajé* in Bahia is a must-try but can be very spicy and heavy on palm oil (*dendê*), which can upset sensitive stomachs.
- **Kilogram (Kilo) Restaurants**: These are a Brazilian staple. You fill your plate from a buffet and pay by the weight. They are an excellent way to try many different dishes cheaply and quickly.

### Dining Etiquette
- **Lunch is the Main Meal**: Brazilians usually eat a heavy lunch around 12:30 or 1:00 PM and a lighter dinner later in the evening (8:00 PM or later).
- **Service**: Most restaurants include a 10-13% service charge. You don't need to tip more.
- **The "Napkin" Culture**: Use napkins to pick up finger foods.
