# Safety & Security: A Practical Guide for American Travelers

Safety is often the number one concern for Americans visiting Brazil. While it is true that Brazil has higher crime rates than most US cities, the vast majority of visits are trouble-free. The key is to shift from a "tourist" mindset to a "street-smart" mindset.

### The Golden Rule: "Não Dê Bobeira"
Brazilians have a phrase, *não dê bobeira*, which translates roughly to "don't be a fool" or "don't give a chance." This means not making yourself an easy target.
- **Phones**: This is the most common target. Do not walk with your phone in your hand. If you need to check a map or call an Uber, step inside a shop or pharmacy to do it.
- **Jewelry**: Leave expensive watches, wedding rings, and necklaces in the hotel safe. Even "costume" jewelry that *looks* expensive can attract unwanted attention.
- **Bags**: Wear backpacks on your front in crowded areas (like the Rio Metro or downtown markets).

### Safe Neighborhoods vs. No-Go Zones
In major cities, safety varies block by block.
- **Rio de Janeiro**: Stick to the "Zona Sul" (South Zone). **Ipanema** and **Leblon** are the safest and most upscale. **Copacabana** is iconic but busier and requires more vigilance, especially at night. Avoid entering any "Favela" unless you are on a reputable, pre-arranged tour.
- **São Paulo**: **Jardins**, **Itaim Bibi**, and **Vila Madalena** are excellent choices for Americans. Be extremely cautious in the "Centro" (Old Downtown) area, especially after business hours, as it can become very desolate and unsafe.

### Common Scams to Avoid
- **The "Good Samaritan"**: Someone might point out a "stain" on your clothes (which they likely sprayed on) and offer to help you clean it while an accomplice picks your pocket.
- **The Beach Distraction**: On beaches like Copacabana, a group might start a small commotion or game near you. While you watch, someone else grabs your bag. Never leave your belongings unattended on the sand.
- **Card Skimming**: Always check ATMs for loose parts. Prefer ATMs inside bank branches during daylight hours rather than standalone machines in dark corners.

### Health Precautions
- **Vaccinations**: The CDC recommends Hepatitis A and Typhoid for most travelers. **Yellow Fever** is recommended for most of Brazil; check if your specific itinerary includes "risk areas" (most of the interior and now parts of the coast).
- **Mosquito-Borne Illnesses**: Zika, Dengue, and Chikungunya are present. Use a repellent with at least 25% DEET. Dengue is currently a significant concern in many urban areas during the rainy season.
- **Water**: **Do not drink tap water.** Stick to bottled or filtered water. Even in high-end hotels, tap water is for showering only.

### Emergency Information
If you are in immediate danger, dial these numbers:
- **Police**: 190
- **Ambulance (SAMU)**: 192
- **Fire Department**: 193
- **Tourist Police (DEAT)**: In Rio and São Paulo, there are specialized police stations for tourists with English-speaking officers.
- **US Embassy (Brasília)**: +55 (61) 3312-7000
- **US Consulate (Rio)**: +55 (21) 3823-2000
- **US Consulate (São Paulo)**: +55 (11) 3250-5000

### Travel Insurance
Your US health insurance likely does not cover international medical evacuations or private Brazilian hospitals. We highly recommend **World Nomads** or **SafetyWing**. Ensure your policy specifically covers "Adventure Sports" if you plan on hang gliding in Rio or trekking in the Amazon.
